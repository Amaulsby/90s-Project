The Evolution of Hacker Culture

Hacking culture is a phenomenon that has been around much longer than the internet. Revolving around curiosity, problem solving, and counterculture, hacking has a deep, rich history and evolution. The article "A Brief History of Hacking" by Jacob Fox sums up the dawn of both this term and culture nicely. While some may point to 1878 as first case of "hacking", when young phone operators pranked callers by switching telephone lines, the term originates from around the 1950's-60's when students at MIT started altering train sets, and were deemed "hackers". But what most people consider true hacking started with John Draper in 1965, a man more famously known as Cap'n Crunch. Draper realized that by using a toy whistle from a cereal box, due to the frequency it produced, he could trick the telephone system and make free long-distance calls. Bypassing technology with cleverness, this is the foundation of hacking.

"Phreaking" as a term comes from the words phone, free, and freak, according to the history of the word from Britannica. The first ever "phreak" was a blind man named Joe Engressia who could whistle a perfect 2600 MHz pitch, recreating the exact frequency of a telephone routing signal. Engressia's friend, John Draper, found that a whistle from a Captain Crunch box produced this very same frequency. This technique spread fast, and eventually "phone phreaks" switched from toy whistles to blue boxes, homemade transmitters generating the same 12 tones used by phone operators. But phreaking was about more than just making free calls, it was about curiosity and the ability to circumvent complex systems. Phreaks weren't just phreaking because they couldn't afford calls, it was a statement about being clever enough to ask "how does this work, and how can I get around it?". While the eventual upgrade of telephone lines in 1983 would effectively end phreaking, the attention it garnered and subsequent movement it started would never be stopped. Once computers and modems became more accessible, this same mindset moved from phone lines to online system.

By the 80s and early 90s, underground forums that predated the web began to arise. These bulletin board systems (BBS) were networks of local computers that could connect via modem and share messages, download files, or even play games. Preserved today on textfiles.com, Douglas Thomas argues in his book Hacker Culture that these boards, available only via dial-up, were the birth of digital identity. Each BBS had its own culture, rules, and hierarchies, with contributors known just by their username and reputation. These boards are perfect snapshots of how hackers learned, traded techniques, and built reputation.

Another hack regarding phone systems, an entry listed as "Aspen.txt" and titled "A Complete Guide To Hacking and Use of ASpEN Voice Mail Systems" was written by "Caveman" and published on January 13th, 1992 by "R.A.W Productions". Caveman explains that ASpEN or Automated Speech Exchange Networks are voicemail systems used by small businesses for individual employees away from their desk. It goes on to explain, with much profanity, how to find, hack, and avoid getting removed from an AsPEN box. Finally, writing how to contact him, shouting out some of his acquaintances, and listing the boards he is a part of, Caveman ends the entry with:

[Disclamer]
I could say that I don't want anyone to do anything contained in this file,
and that it is strictly for informational purposes, but I know that people
are going to go out and do this shit. So, Mr. Law Enforcement Agent, cram it,
you can't find me, for all you know, I could be your father (and knowing your
mother, I probably am.) AMF.

[Bullshit]
If you have suggestions, comments, or have nothing better to do, you can
reach me (Caveman) at [Legion 1]: 202.337.2844 [12/24/96/14.4]

[PEACE]
Peace to Mr. Black, my partner in crime.
Peace to Tomellicas, Legion creator.
Peace to G-Spot, for finding the systems to hack on.
Peace to Night Ranger, for his article.

[The Boards]
Legion 1 [202]
Powerdome, INC. [703]
Midian Private [703]
School For Scandal [301]

This type of post perfectly sums up hacker culture in the 90s. Building a name for himself, working with other hackers, sharing his methods, and sticking it to both law enforcement and businessmen, Caveman's entry is the quintessential "hacker" post. But hacking, especially by the 80s and 90s, was about much more than just tricking phone systems. When the Web arrived, it did so with little to no security measures, trusting users blindly. Well seasoned hackers, like Caveman, were likely quite excited that this was the case. To hackers, the Web was a new playground, one with thousands of vulnerable sites hosted from university servers, basement Linux machines, and business offices, and all accessible from the comfort of your own home.

WHAT MADE THE EARLY WEB INSECURE?

Originally, websites were designed with completely static HTML, which meant there was no interactivity. Because of this, Common Gateway Interface (CGI) scripts were written in Perl, C, or Shell scripts to enable interaction with the server. However, CGI was a disaster for security, unsanitized input strings were sent directly to scripts and developers went with the blanket assumption that users would only give normal input. This meant that anyone with some programming knowledge could end up with full access to a server via a "poisoned" input.

At the time, most "System Administrators" were just young college graduates who had a knack for computers, not security experts (which didn't exist as a job yet). This meant that most admins just used sample configurations for their sites, and implemented CGI with a myriad of vulnerabilities. One of the first famous hacks exploiting CGI was the PHF exploit. By the mid 90's, thousands of servers came preinstalled with PHF, a CGI phone book lookup script. PHF took user input and passed it directly into shell command on the server. As explained in a writeup archived on insecure.org, access, sometimes even root access, to any server running PHF could be attained by opening a telnet port (a connection protocol) on your machine and simply pasting (with minor adjustments according to your scenario) this into the site:

GET
/cgi-bin/phf?Jserver=foobar.com%0Acat%20/etc/ passwd%0A&Qalias=&Qname=foo&Qemail=&Qnickname=&Qoffice_phone=&Qcallsign=&Qproxy=&Qhigh_school=&Qslip= HTTP/1.0
Accept: */*
Accept: application/x-wais-source
Accept: text/plain
Accept: text/html
Accept: www/mime
User-Agent: Lynx/2.3 BETA libwww/2.14
Referer: http://localhost/cgi-bin/phf

These types of exploits led to thousands of compromised servers, and was the first example of widespread remote injection of arbitrary code over HTTP. While CGI didn't appear until the 90's, earlier hacks exploiting machines like this led to the passing of one of the most important laws in hacking history, the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA) of 1986. This law criminalized unauthorized access to computer systems, something that wasn't really explicitly defined, and broadly made it easy to prosecute hackers regardless of how much harm was actually done. Regardless of this, hackers understood at the time that the exploits possible showed the naivety of developers. The Web trusted users by default, a sentiment that would be true much longer than CGI was used, and would continue to lead to more exploitation. Once this was clear, hackers realized that most systems could be breached one way or another, it was just a puzzle waiting to be solved. And conveniently, hackers love puzzles.

THE DEVELOPMENT OF MODERN DATABASES AND THEIR EXPLOITATION

By the mid to late 90's, many dynamic sites began using early SQL servers. SQL, or Structured Query Language, is a tool used to define, manage, and query data stored in a database. Moreover, SQL allows users to interact with structured data, such as by creating a username and password that is then stored on the server. SQL is still widely used today, and is the standard language for relational databases. However, modern SQL is taught with much better security practices, unlike the sites of the 90s. When SQL first began being implemented, devlopers assumed users didnt have access to the database via the website, so it was safe to assume that only a user would know, and attempt to input, their own password...right?

Well, in reality early SQL was written very poorly. This isn't necessarily the fault of the programmers, as this was simply the accepted way to write code at the time, but it created many exploitable issues. For instance, early SQL developers concatenated strings with user input by writing a script like:

SELECT * FROM users WHERE username = '$user' AND password = '$pass'

This script essentially says: Get some information from the database (SELECT), get everything from the matching row (*), look inside the table called users (FROM users), but only give me the rows that meet the following conditions (WHERE). Next it says, find the rows where the username column matches the user input (username = '$user, if the user typed admin then username = 'admin'), then both conditions must be true (AND), find rows where the password column matches the user input (password = $pass). This essentially means if a row matches with the username and password, access for that user is granted.

This code works, and seems to be pretty intuitive, logging in a user when and only when they input the correct password. However, the big problem here is that the database cannot tell where the input from the user ends and SQL code begins. This introduces the concept of SQL injection. In SQL, '1' = '1' is an example of an always true statement. If a hacker were to input the admin username but the password as:

' ' OR '1' = '1'

The SQL query would now become:

SELECT * FROM users WHERE username = 'admin' AND password = ' ' OR '1' = '1'

This would evaluate to:

1: password = ' ' (false)
2: '1' = '1' (true)

Making the entire statement true and logging the hacker in as the admin.

In layman's terms, the hacker is telling the database "log me in as admin if the password is blank or if 1 = 1." and since 1 always equals 1, access is granted. This exact attack is possible on this very website, and if " or ""=" is pasted into the login page, the user will be able to access the admin account (first account in the database) without a password. Another attack that is also possible on this website is one that destroys the database instead of dropping it. For hackers that want to wreak havoc instead of infiltrate an account, one could enter either into the username or password field:

'; DROP TABLE users;--

This code turns anything following it into a comment with "--", meaning anything follow "DROP TABLE users" will not be executed. It then deletes the entire table of users with "DROP TABLE users" and ignores everything after.

Similarly, an attacker could write SQL injections that display the entire table of usernames and passwords. Since most early websites didn't hash passwords, or hashed them using poor algorithms, this could expose sensitive information. Furthermore, if a site stored information such as name, phone number, address, username, password, and possibly even financial information, this could all be accessed with a simple SQL injection attack.

Hence, the fundamental flaw of early SQL was, again, that it trusts the user to only have normal input, and trusts whatever they type to be fed directly to the database. Modern SQL treats user input as data, rather than a part of the command. This is done using parameterized queries, where instead of concatenating strings together, the program will send the user code separately from the SQL command. This is done so that the user input cannot escape into the actual database, with inescapable placeholders representing user input. Below is an example of the modern, safe version of the same SQL query:

SELECT * FROM users WHERE username = ? AND password = ?
cursor.execute(query, (username, password)) -> Python implementation used in this site (but with unsafe SQL)

This flavor of hack completely changed the game. With one simple input, attackers could gain complete access to servers and do with it what they please. This, along with other hacks of the time, changed the culture around hacking and specifically the reputation of hackers. Instead of posting techniques to build a reputation, a hacker could just plaster their handle all over a popular website, telling everyone they took total control.

THE CRACKDOWN ON HACKERS, AND SPLIT OF THE INDUSTRY

Numerous hacks ranging from "put my name on the website" to "steal everyone's credit card" attacks put hackers into the spotlight. Hollywood portrayed them as mischevious villains, and the average internet user came to fear them. In 1998 the Digital Millenium Copyright Act (DMCA) was passed which made it illegal to "circumvent technological protection measures", aimed at cracking down on piracy and DVD cracking. However, this changed the way hackers could share vulnerabilities, because publishing a way to bypass a digital lock (even for legitimate study) could be considered criminal. Following 9/11, the Patriot Act of 2001 took this further and broadened the definition of a "protected machine" while making penalties for intrusions greater. With cybercrime now considered a matter of national security, and government agencies gaining new surveillance powers, hacking quickly became associated with terrorism, espionage, and criminal mischief.

These developments caused a schism in hacker culture. Many hackers went towards the professional side, becoming "white hat hackers" or security professionals researching vulnerabilities or "penetration testing" companies sites. "Gray hat hackers" took the middleground, finding vulnerabilities but selling them to companies in order to be fixed via programs known as "bug bounty programs", a practice that would have been a felony a decade prior. Those who chose to become "black hat hackers" continued exploiting, selling vulnerabilities to the highest bidder, and using hacking either to make money illegally or push a political agenda.

While young, curious, and clever 80s-90s hackers grew into security consultants, bug hunters, or even serious criminals, the world and culture of hacking changed drastically. However, many things remain similar to this day. The late 90s and early 2000s saw the rise of hacking get-togethers in the form of local educational meetups, and eventually B-side conferences, volunteer-run conventions focused on cutting edge cybersecurity topics. Many of these conferences are still around today, most notably DEFCON, and consist of tool showcases, hacking competitions (often Capture The Flag, or CTF, themed games), and technical talks. These conferences cater to all hackers, but predominately are places where white and grey hat hackers can talk shop, make connections, and show off their skills. Conferences like this shaped modern hacker culture, instead of clandestine message boards hackers could meet up to share ideas and conduct real-world exercises together. This led to a much more open, accessible network of cybersecurity, still focused on solving puzzles, but doing so in a challenge-oriented, isolated, and competitive manner rather than hacking unsuspecting machines. This isn't to say that hackers who might be considered black hats don't attend these conferences or share their knowledge, but rather that the mainstream hacker culture today is more centered around a transparent, educational environment ultimately geared at improving security.

This era also saw the rise of the "Dark Web", a series of "hidden services" only accessible using a secure, fairly anonymous routing protocol known as The Onion Router (TOR). Completely decentralized and "free-reign", the dark web is filled with plaintext sites valuing security over anything else. It is also full of hackers, criminals, and government honey pots, with sites catering to drug users, cybercriminals, and even pedophiles. However, the dark web is not an entirely negative place, though often only associated with crime, and provides many wholesome services such as allowing access to censored news/information for people under totalitarian regimes. In the world of hacking, the dark web is a place where you can buy, sell, and view new exploits, data leaks, or compromising information. Very reminiscent of old BBSes and the early internet, the dark web contains message boards, chat rooms, and marketplaces for all sorts of illicit services and products. While black hats might be using the dark web to sell their work or buy a leak, white and grey hat hackers often have to use it as well whether to verify validity of data breaches or run interference on a hacked company's behalf. Governments around the world use TOR as well, with the CIA having used it to communicate with spies, and agencies like the FBI or NSA using it to create honey pots sites to collect information on cybercriminals.

MODERN BLACKHATS AND ORGANIZED CYBERCRIME

While white and grey hat hackers became common, necessary, and respectable occupations, the world of black hat hacking continued to evolve as well. In fact, black hat hacking today might be closer to what Hollywood in the 90s and early 2000s portrayed it as than what hackers back then were actually doing. Except, rather than the lone "mischevious hacker in the basement", cybercrime today is dominated by organized, elite ransomware cartels, often suspected to be backed by nation states or greater organized crime. ransomware attacks consist of breaking into a system and systematically locking out all of its users, typically by encrypting huge amounts of data. The attackers will then demand a large sum of money in a certain amount of time, in difficult to trace cryptocurrency, and force the victim to hope that once they pay it their files will be released. If the victim doens't pay, or waits too long, the data is completely destroyed. Typically, with some exceptions, the hackers live up to their word. This makes it clear to possible future victims that "this isn't a joke or scam, we will destroy your data" while also creating an incentive to pay by historically sticking to their word.

This type of hack is extremely lucrative, and it is no coincidence that these attacks are carried out by well-funded, structured teams, with budgets and strategic goals. These teams often target systems that are crucial to safety or commerce, such as hospitals, business POS systems, and in one instance even a cargo facility. While many of the exploits they use still trace back to the fundamental concepts hackers of the 90s discovered, such as putting too much trust in the user, comparing early hackers to modern ransomware groups is like comparing an armed bank robber to an elite team of well-trained thieves. A 2020 New York Times article titled "Ransomware Attacks Grow, Crippling Cities and Businesses" by Nathaniel Popper states that

"The average payment to release files spiked to $84,116 in the last quarter of 2019, more than double what it was the previous quarter...In the last month of 2019, that jumped to $190,946, with several organizations facing ransom demands in the millions of dollars."
With these types of payouts, there is no question why ransomware attacks continue to become more frequent. While the big teams go for the big fish, such as crucial, high-volume computer systems, they also make money from selling their ransomware software to other hackers. Requiring little to no technical experience, a purchase made on the dark web with cryptocurrency could instantly provide you with an all-in-one ransomware system ready to be deployed at your workplace, school, or local hospital, with software often written to send some of the ransom money to the purchaser, and some as a kickback to the developer(s).

But ransomware attacks don't just target business and infrastructure. Many smaller-time hackers focus on personally encrypting an individuals machine, demanding a smaller payout to save their work files, family photos, or even explicit content that the hacker might threaten to release if unpaid. The New York Times also states that security experts say our current numbers on ransomware attacks are underestimated, considering most individuals just pay the few hunred dollars to get their files back quietly, never reporting the incident to authorities.

While ransomware is the most lucrative and fastest-growing type of attack today, black hat hackers still make money other ways. Selling lists of passwords, banking information, or even specifically curated exploits are all common ways a black hat hacker might make money. While black hats are often grouped in with ransomware organizations and well-trained teams of cybercriminals, in reality most of them are just curious, clever computer nerds who are willing to break the law to make a few bucks doing something they love. While it's unlikely you might see a member of a state-sponsored ramsomware organization at a hacker conference, there's a good chance you'll run into some mischevious small-time hacker who's username is jotted down on an FBI list somewhere.

MY PERSONAL HACKING STORIES & FINAL REMARKS

While hacking has a fascinating history and culture surrounding it, there is nothing quite like experiencing it firsthand. I myself was lucky enough to start a Tik-Tok in 2020 revolving around cybersecurity. 16-year old me had gotten into CTF-style hacking, social engineering labs, and the occasional poke around local WiFI networks (with consent of course). Some of the stuff I was doing was just super cool to me, so I figured I'd share it with others who might have similar interests. What I did not expect was to gain over 500,000 followers in less than 6 months, and become the face (though I never did show my face), of a short-lived hacker community during the Covid-19 pandemic. At its height, my Discord server was a 5000 person army consisting of real-world white hat professionals, teenagers and script kiddies begging to learn how to become movie-style hackers, Instagram models asking people to log them back into their accounts, and of course a bunch of mischevious computer nerds, like myself at the time, constantly one-upping each other and pushing the limits of what my volunteer moderation team would allow.

I am proud to say I taught many people how to improve their account and data security, OPSEC (Operational Security), and become real life hackers with the educational resources available online today. I am a little less proud to say that while interesting, the experience was quite daunting at times. My server (but not myself) was mentioned briefly in an official FBI document one of my buddies requested via FOIA, but was linked to a user who didn't contribute much but was apparently a well connected black hat. My daily routine at the time also consisted of checking to see if i was doxxed on doxbin, a site where anonymous users post the personal information of people online, creating sock puppet accounts of fake people who shared information with me, and defending myself to hundreds of hackers claiming I was a fraud and they were more skilled. While quiet today, I met some of my best online friends during that time, some of who run their own cybersecurity companies today.

The best part about this experience wasn't the idea of having a 5000-idiot strong hacker army ready to deploy at my beckon call, though that was pretty awesome, but was experiencing the true world of hacking right from the center of it. Staying up until 4 in the morning while a guy from the Czech Republic is showing 40 people how to scrape IP addresses of improperly setup Malaysian security cameras is truly a novel thing. While hacking has changed drastically since the 90s, this collaborative culture has always remained the cornerstone. New vulnerabilities will be found, companies and legislators will respond to attacks, and hackers will adapt accordingly, but hacking as a culture will always be about curiosity, problem solving, and just the right amount of mischief.

DESCRIPTIONS OF A FEW TERMS

Hacking, more so than almost any other environment, is dominated by a myriad of acronyms and odd terms. Here I will expand on some of the more popular of these and where they originated from.

  • Script Kiddie

    • Someone who uses pre-written exploits to engage in hacking without understanding how or why they work. The term originated in the early 90s on cybersecurity mailing lists. Hackers will often use this word frequently, referring to anyone calling themselves a hacker as a script kiddie because it is a simple insult that really gets under their skin.

  • Leet, 1337, or Leetspeak

    • This refers to internet slang susbtituting numbers and symbols for letters, originating in the 1980s. A hacker's proficiency in leetspeak was often seen to be indicative of their skill and presence in hacker spaces

  • Root / Rooting a system

    • Root access is another term for the highest level of access to a system, typically a UNIX based system. This term originated in the 1970s but became mainstream in the 90s.

  • 0wn / pwn

    • Similar to rooting, to 0wn or pwn means to dominate, compromise, or take control of a system. This originated in the mid to late 90s, as hackers and gamers alike would misspell own as 0wn or pwn as the keys are adjacent.

  • Zero-day / 0-day

    • A zero day is simply a vulnerability that is newly discovered and unpatched (hence, zero days to fix it). Originating in the mid 90s, 0-day was original used to refer to newly cracked software shared on BBS boards.

  • Backdoor

    • While often picked up by Hollywood as a "golden ticket"-esque entry point for a hacker, backdoors are a genuine method of accessing systems without authentication. Originating in the 70s but becoming popular in the 80s, these are essentially entry points often left intentionally by developers (or placed there by attackers with access) that are hidden and accessed at a later point.

  • Social Engineering

    • The act of hacking people, rather than technology. The term was popularized in the 1980s with Kevin Mitnick, and is still a pillar of hacking today arguably more important than technical prowess. Phishing emails are a basic example of social engineering, with a more advanced example being high level fraud such as sim-swapping to gain access to someones phone and make calls on their behalf. Tricking even a waiter to give you your food for free is social engineering.

  • DoS / DDoS (Denial of Service

    • An attack often perpetrated by a large network of computers controlled by malware, aiming to overload a system or network with constant requests until it crashes. This term was first seen in the 80s, but was popularized in the 90s when attacks like this became more prevalent.

  • Botnet

    • A network of computers infected by malware, able to be controlled remotely to carry out tasks. Originating from the mid-late 90s, botnets have been known to be used to carry out DDoS attacks as well as mine cryptocurrency more recently.

  • IRC (Internet Relay Chat)

    • First deployed in Finland in 1988 and popularized in the 90s, these chatrooms were real-time text chats used by hackers, IRCs can still be found today and are also fairly easy to host on your own.

  • DEFCON

    • One of the largest hacker conventions, started in 1993 by Jeff Moss, AKA "Dark Tangent", and hosted in Las Vegas. While a completely legal conference, DEFCON attendees recommend not bringing your smartphone, laptop, or credit card.
